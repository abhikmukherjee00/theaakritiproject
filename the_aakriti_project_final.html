Beyond the Brush: Deciphering the Lithic Lineage and Ritual ‘Writing’ of Pithora in Chota Udepur

Author: Abhik Mukherjee

Affiliation: Masters student (FYMA), Maharaja Sayajirao University, Baroda

abhikmukherjee43@gmail.com

Foreword

In the tribal heartland of Chota Udepur, Gujarat, a Pithora writing is frequently misread by the modern gaze as a primitive decorative art. This project challenges such reductionist labels by repositioning the Pithora as a sophisticated system of ritual "writing", a sacred semiotic device central to the devotional life of the Rathwa community. Drawing on original fieldwork, this study documents the district's prehistoric rock art sites to establish an ethnoarchaeological lineage between ancient lithic expressions and contemporary writings.

The research argues that the Pithora is an ontological map rather than a visual ornament. Through ethnographic documentation of the ritual process, from the invocation by the Badwa (priest) to the precise execution by the Lakhara (writer), the project illustrates how the writing functions as a living archive of Rathwa cosmology and social hierarchy. By analysing specific symbols, such as the stylized horses to motifs of everyday life the study unveils a sacred literacy that bridges the gap between the petroglyphs of the past and the mud walls of the present.

Central to this thesis is the community perspective: the Pithora is a "vow-fulfillment" mechanism where the act of "writing" the wall is a transformative spiritual labour. By shifting the scholarly gaze from "art history" to "ritual literacy," this project seeks to correct the common misconception of Pithora as a mere painting. Instead, it frames the tradition as a resilient regional "school" of visual communication that serves as the primary vessel for collective memory and social cohesion.

Keywords: Rathwa Community, Chota Udepur, Pithora Ritual, Vow-Fulfillment Mechanism, Sacred Semiotics, Ritual Literacy.

The Rathwas, their Pithora tradition and a semiotic analysis

The Rathwa are a Scheduled Tribe historically concentrated in the hill and forest tracts of Chhota Udepur and adjoining talukas of eastern Gujarat, with links extending into the neighbouring border regions of Madhya Pradesh. Their designation is commonly explained through the term rathbistar, meaning an inhabitant of a forested or hilly region, which reflects both their ecological setting and the historical geography of their settlement. In ethnographic literature, they are often described as a tribal community with a distinct dialect of Gujarati, known as Rathwi, and with cultural affinities to other Adivasi populations of the western Indian plateau.

The cultural life of the Rathwa is especially known for “Pithora ritual writing,” one of the most distinctive forms of tribal religious expression in western India. Pithora paintings are commissioned in fulfilment of vows made to Baba Pithora, the central protective deity of the community, usually in response to illness, crop failure, infertility, or other forms of crisis.

The Mythological Genesis of Baba Pithora

Baba Pithora is the supreme deity of the Rathwa, embodying universal creation, grain, and household protection. His backstory begins with Baba Ind, the lord of rains, and his sisters, including Rani Kali Koyal and Rani Kajal.

Pithora was conceived out of wedlock following an encounter between Kali Koyal and Kandu Raja. Fearing social wrath, Kali Koyal abandoned the infant on a lotus leaf in the Ganga-Jamuna. He was rescued by Rani Kajal, who raised him on the milk of the Akdo tree and floral juices, providing him with a celestial education.

Study Area & The Act of Writing

The ethnographic research utilized a systematic field survey across Chota Udepur, employing qualitative methods like structured interviews and questionnaires. Data collection targeted three key stakeholder groups to capture the socio-religious framework of the Pithora tradition:

  • Village Communities: Perspectives on communal preservation.
  • Lakharas (Painters): Artisanal and material culture insights.
  • Badwas (Priests): Sacred and theological documentation.

A Ghardhanis Perspective

The ritual installation of a Pithora writing is a profound spiritual undertaking for the Rathwa community, transforming a home into a sacred site. For a Ghardhani (homeowner), the Pithora is a "living domestic covenant,” the fulfillment of a Mannat (vow) taken during survival crises like illness, livestock loss, or crop failure.

A Lakharas Perspective

In the Rathwa community, the Lakhara (ritual scribe) is not merely an artisan but a vital intercessor between the human and spiritual realms. As explained by Padma Shri awardee Mr. Paresh Rathwa, the creation of a Pithora writing is the fulfillment of a Mannat, transforming a wall into a Devsthana (abode for the gods).

A Badwas Perspective & The Ritual Night

Within the Rathwa society, the Badwa transcends the definitions of a common priest or healer; he is the metaphysical architect of the community's survival. The climax occurs on Wednesday night during a singing session accompanied by Dhak or Damru drums. This psycho-acoustic environment induces a trance state called Pawan, where Pithoradeva enters the Badwa’s body.

A Semiotic Interpretation of the Pithora Writing

The motifs in a Pithora painting are a sophisticated visual language that blends Rathwa mythology with the pragmatism of daily life. The painting is not a static artwork but a dynamic representation of the universe.

The Divine Assembly

Abho Kunbi and Mathari

This farming couple created the art of agriculture. They are credited with teaching humanity how to till, sow, and harvest. Their presence in a Pithora writing anchors the Pithora in the physical labour of subsistence.

Lakhari and Jokhari

Portrayed sitting on highchairs, these "authors of the future" visit a child on the fifth or sixth day of birth to write their destiny. They are credited with writing Pithora’s future on the wall, thereby initiating the very tradition the Lakharas continue.

Purvaj na Panch Ghoda (The Ancestral Horses)

These five horses represent the community's ancestors, known as Khatrij Dev. In daily life, Rathwas place wooden carvings of these horses in farms or homes. In the painting, they are the first to be worshipped, ensuring the family remains connected to its lineage.

Motifs of Nature & The Animal Kingdom

Elements of nature are treated as living deities. The Sun (Huriyadev) and Moon (Handaryo Dev), sacred trees like the Kadam tree, and animals representing strength, righteousness, and ritual necessity play vital roles.

Human Activities and Social Guardians

The writing acts as a mirror to Rathwa society, depicting the Badwa conducting ceremonies, and daily labour such as churning buttermilk, drawing water from handpumps or the making of alcohol. Specific deities and guardians are also portrayed:

  • Chowkidars (guardians): Originally shown with bows and now with guns, placed strategically to protect the "writing."
  • Nakti Bhuten: A fierce deity on a white horse placed on the far left. He is the village protector; his name must be invoked before entering the village to ward off malevolent spirits.
  • Kamadeva: A figure of structural importance included to ensure the continuation of the lineage and the birth of offspring.

Modernity and Manokamna

A striking feature of contemporary Pithora paintings is the inclusion of "modern" motifs: trucks, buses, trains, clocks, and rifles. While critics see this as a dilution of tradition, Lakharas explain it through Manokamna (desire). As the Rathwa world changes, their prayers and desires evolve.

Ethnoarchaeological Continuities between Rock Art and the Pithora Tradition

Rock art refers to human-made markings, primarily petroglyphs (carvings) and pictographs (paintings), placed on natural stone surfaces. Archaeologist Robert Bednarik (2014) defines rock art as exograms, which are externalized memory traces. Unlike "engrams" stored in the brain, exograms allow humans to store and transmit complex symbolic information across generations outside of biological memory.

In India, rock art provides a continuous record from the Upper Paleolithic to the Historical period. This study systematically documents rock art across Gujarat’s Chota Udepur district, focusing on sites like Chitra Dungri and Kikawada (Ghoda Lekhan, Kikawada village base, Mandva Sil). Situated within the Vindhya and Satpura ranges, these shelters feature pictographs in red, white, ochre, and black. The imagery, comprising animals, geometric patterns, and hunting scenes, offers vital insights into the region's paleo-environmental and socio-cultural history.

The Chota Udepur district in Gujarat is a profound cultural intersection where lithic vestiges merge with the living ritual life of the Rathwas. This region serves as an extraordinary ethnoarchaeological laboratory, suggesting that the sacred rock art of the hills is not a defunct epoch, but the foundational layer of a ritual landscape manifested today in Pithora wall paintings. By examining motifs, ritual materiality, and spatial consumption, we discern a belief trajectory that has evolved from hunter-gatherer subsistence to complex tribal cosmologies, rooted in ancestral veneration and ecological reciprocity.

I) Parallels in Visual Symbology: The Evolution of Motifs

The transition from rock surfaces at sites like Ghoda Lekhan, Mandva Sil, and Chitra Dungri to the plastered walls of domestic dwellings involves a recontextualization of symbols that have retained their significance for millennia.

Equine Centrality: From Rock Art to Pithoradeva

The horse is the most dominant motif in both records. At Ghoda Lekhan, early renderings in red and white pigments suggest an early veneration of the animal. In the modern Pithora tradition, this reaches its zenith; the horse is no longer just a biological entity but the primary vessel for the divine. Pithora paintings are considered written rather than painted, signifying their role as a record of divine presence. Baba Pithora is always depicted on a horse, and the ritual itself is initiated to fulfil a vow to him.

Evolution of the Guardian: The Chowkidar

An ethnoarchaeological parallel is found in the transition of the Chowkidar (guardian). Archaeological evidence at Kikawada shows figures carrying traditional swords and shields. In contemporary Pithora paintings, this role has been updated to include human figures with guns, representing policemen or soldiers. This integration of modern elements, a process of "heritagisation.” demonstrates the Rathwa’s ability to absorb the tools of the present into an ancient spiritual framework without diminishing ritual value.

Ecological Realism and the Naturalistic Landscape

Naturalistic portrayals of fauna at Chitra Dungri and Mandva Sil include humped bulls, peacocks, and chickens. In Pithora writings, these are essential to creating an Akho Pithoro (a complete Pithora), representing the totality of the universe. Humped Bulls are linked to the farming couple Abho Kunbi and Mathari, ensuring livestock prosperity. Peacocks represent fertility and serve as vehicles for goddesses, while Chickens provide the historical anchor for contemporary sacrificial rituals.

II) Parallels in Ritual Materiality and Offerings

The objects used in rituals today mirror images found in the archaeological record, suggesting that the act of "offering" has evolved from a painted representation into a three-dimensional artifact.

The Terracotta Lineage: Ritual Horse Offerings

A profound continuity is the offering of clay horses to Pithoradeva. In the Kumbharwada (potters' lane), families have maintained this craft for generations. Potters like Rakeshbhai and Dayabhai Kumbhara describe the clay horse as a vessel that carries prayers to the celestial realm. These craftsmen also produce kodiyu (lamps) and deras (miniature shrines), translating two-dimensional rock imagery into three-dimensional ritual objects.

Avian sacrifices: The Chicken from Mandva Sil to Pithora

The depiction of chickens at Mandva Sil provides a historical anchor for the contemporary sacrifice of chickens overseen by the Badwa (shaman) during the Makai ni Vidhi (blessing of grains).

Martial Offerings

Similarly, painitings of figures with swords and shields at Kikawada represent objects that are now sanctified. In the Pithora ritual, swords are placed near the wall as symbolic weapons for the gods, reflecting the transformation of a tool of warfare into a sanctified emblem of guardianship. In some rituals, the Badva may even use a sword to perform specific cleansing motions, mirroring the posture of the ancient warriors depicted in the rock shelters of Chota Udepur.

The bow and arrow in Chhota Udepur represent a definitive case of ethnoarchaeological continuity, evolving through three distinct phases: subsistence, warfare, and modern ceremonial use. Today, while the bow is no longer a primary hunting tool or a weapon of resistance, it remains a vital ritual offering at the base of the Pithora wall to honour the Khatrij Dev (ancestors), seeking success in contemporary "hunts" such as harvest and prosperity.

III) Spatial Consumption: From Cave Walls to the Domestic Hearth

Ancient rock art is typically found on cave ceilings, locations offering "otherworldliness." Locals color-coded these sites as Dhorabhutoni Ghodi (white paintings) and Lalbhutoni Ghodi (red paintings). The Pithora shifts this "privatization" of the ritual to the interior walls of the home, specifically the wall dividing the osari (veranda) from the kitchen.

However, the preparation of space maintains an ethnoarchaeological parallel. The ancient artists selected specific stone textures; similarly, the Rathwa prepare walls with Lipai (cow dung, water, and Pandu or white clay). This seven-day process performed by unmarried girls essentially creates a purified, rock-like surface for divine "writing." The "sacred enclosure" of the writing mimics the natural boundaries of a cave.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the Pithora tradition of Chota Udepur transcends the narrow modern classification of "folk art," revealing itself instead as a sophisticated and resilient system of ritual literacy. By repositioning the Pithora as a "sacred writing" rather than a mere decorative mural, this study highlights its role as a living archive of Rathwa cosmology, social hierarchy, and collective memory.

Ultimately, shifting the scholarly gaze from "art history" to "ritual literacy" provides a more authentic framework for understanding the Pithora, not as a relic of a "primitive" past, but as a sophisticated, proprietary language that continues to bridge the gap between ancient petroglyphs and the living present.
